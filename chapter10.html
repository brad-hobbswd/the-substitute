Chapter 10

What Every Addiction Has in Common

As my understanding of addiction continued to grow, I found myself becoming less interested in the differences between addictions and more interested in their similarities. Early on, it seemed natural to place addictions into separate categories. Alcohol belonged in one category. Drugs belonged in another. Gambling, pornography, shopping, food, work, and countless other compulsive behaviors appeared to exist within their own unique worlds. Each carried different consequences, different social perceptions, and different paths toward recovery.

The more stories I encountered, however, the more those distinctions began to feel incomplete. While the substitutes varied dramatically, the deeper struggles often sounded remarkably familiar. Beneath the surface details, people frequently described many of the same emotional experiences. Some spoke of anxiety that never seemed to quiet. Others described loneliness that followed them through every season of life. Many carried unresolved grief, lingering shame, fear of rejection, feelings of inadequacy, or a persistent sense that something important was missing.

Although their chosen substitutes were different, the underlying search often sounded strikingly similar. People were searching for relief from burdens they no longer wanted to carry. They were looking for comfort in places where comfort seemed available. They were attempting to escape emotional pain, find acceptance, experience peace, or create distance from struggles that felt overwhelming. The substitute changed from person to person, yet the longing beneath the substitute often remained surprisingly consistent.

This realization shifted the way I thought about addiction. Rather than asking only what someone was using, I became increasingly interested in what they hoped to gain from using it. The substance or behavior often represented only part of the story. A deeper understanding required examining the emotional, psychological, and sometimes spiritual needs that made the substitute so attractive in the first place.

When viewed from this perspective, addiction begins to reveal a pattern that extends far beyond any particular substance. Regardless of whether a person turns toward alcohol, gambling, work, relationships, food, or some other substitute, the behavior often serves a similar purpose. The individual is attempting to obtain something that feels important, necessary, or difficult to find elsewhere. Relief from anxiety, freedom from emotional pain, a sense of belonging, temporary comfort, personal significance, or inner peace frequently become part of the search.

For a time, the substitute appears to work. Relief is experienced. Comfort is found. Emotional burdens seem lighter. The individual discovers something that appears capable of providing what has been missing. This is precisely what gives addiction much of its power. The substitute offers enough benefit to create hope, even though that benefit gradually becomes more temporary and increasingly costly.

Over time, a painful shift often occurs. What once felt like a solution slowly becomes another source of suffering. The relief does not last as long as it once did. The comfort becomes more difficult to reach. Greater amounts of the substitute are often needed to achieve the same effect. Relationships become strained. Consequences accumulate. The person continues pursuing the substitute while simultaneously watching its promises become less convincing.

Yet many continue returning because they are not merely pursuing a substance. They are pursuing what the substance once seemed to provide. The memory of relief remains powerful. The hope that peace, comfort, acceptance, or escape can be found again continues drawing them back. This cycle helps explain why addiction can feel so confusing to those observing from the outside. The behavior appears irrational, yet the underlying desire often makes perfect sense.

As I reflected on these realities, another question began demanding attention. If every addiction seems connected to a deeper need, what exactly is that need? What is the person truly searching for? What absence gives the substitute its power? What longing remains unresolved beneath the behavior?

Those questions led me toward one of the most important discoveries in this entire journey. Every substitute appears to point toward something hidden. Every addiction seems to circle around a vacancy that has not yet been fully understood. Until that vacancy is identified, the substitute often retains its influence, regardless of the form it takes.

Understanding addiction therefore requires more than examining the behavior itself. It requires us to look beneath the behavior and ask what is happening in the space the behavior is attempting to fill.

That hidden space is where our journey takes us next.
