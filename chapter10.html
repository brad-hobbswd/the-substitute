Chapter 10

What Every Addiction Has in Common

The deeper I explored addiction, the more I became fascinated by a simple observation. The substances were different. The behaviors were different. The consequences varied from person to person. Yet beneath all the differences, I kept encountering the same pattern.

Alcohol looked different from gambling.

Gambling looked different from compulsive shopping.

Compulsive shopping looked different from pornography.

Pornography looked different from work addiction.

Work addiction looked different from approval seeking.

Yet despite these differences, the underlying story often sounded remarkably familiar.

Someone was looking for relief.

Someone was searching for comfort.

Someone was attempting to escape pain.

Someone was trying to quiet anxiety.

Someone was seeking significance.

Someone was longing for peace.

The substitute changed, but the search remained.

This realization helped me understand why addiction cannot be fully explained by focusing on the object of addiction alone. The substance may tell us something about the person's chosen method of coping, but the substitute rarely tells the entire story. To understand addiction more completely, we must look beyond what the person is using and begin examining what the person is pursuing.

When viewed from this perspective, every addiction appears to involve a transaction. The individual exchanges something in hopes of gaining something else. The exchange may not be conscious, and it may not even be fully understood by the person engaging in it, yet the pattern remains surprisingly consistent.

A person exchanges health for temporary relief.

A person exchanges money for temporary comfort.

A person exchanges relationships for temporary escape.

A person exchanges peace of mind for temporary distraction.

The individual continues making the exchange because the desired benefit feels important enough to justify the cost, at least for a season.

Over time, however, another reality begins to emerge. The cost grows larger while the benefit grows smaller.

The relief does not last as long.

The comfort becomes less satisfying.

The distraction loses effectiveness.

The peace becomes increasingly difficult to reach.

What once seemed like a solution gradually becomes another source of suffering.

This cycle helps explain why addiction can feel so confusing to both the addicted individual and the people who love them. The person continues returning to something that is clearly causing harm, yet the return makes sense when viewed through the lens of the original need. The individual remembers what the substitute once provided and continues hoping the next experience will deliver the same result.

Many people describe addiction as becoming trapped. That description feels appropriate because addiction often begins as a choice and gradually evolves into something far more complicated. The substitute that initially promised freedom eventually creates dependence. The thing that appeared to solve a problem becomes a problem itself.

As I reflected on these patterns, another important truth began to emerge. Every addiction seems to contain a promise.

The promise may never be spoken aloud, yet it is present nonetheless.

The promise says relief is available.

The promise says comfort can be found.

The promise says loneliness can disappear.

The promise says fear can be managed.

The promise says peace is only one more experience away.

For a time, the promise appears believable because the substitute provides enough relief to keep hope alive. The individual continues returning because the possibility of relief feels better than the certainty of pain.

The tragedy is that substitutes can only imitate what they promise. They can create moments of comfort without producing lasting comfort. They can create moments of peace without producing lasting peace. They can create moments of relief without addressing the deeper need that made relief necessary in the first place.

This may be the single most important characteristic shared by every addiction. Regardless of the substitute involved, the deeper need remains largely untouched. The person experiences temporary satisfaction while the underlying hunger continues waiting beneath the surface.

Such understanding changes the way we think about recovery. Recovery is not merely the removal of a substitute. Recovery involves discovering why the substitute became necessary. Recovery involves identifying the need beneath the behavior, the wound beneath the struggle, and the longing beneath the pursuit.

The more I considered this idea, the more I found myself returning to a question that seemed impossible to ignore.

What exactly is missing?

If every addiction points toward a deeper need, then understanding addiction requires understanding the vacancy that gave the substitute its power. The behavior may be visible, yet something hidden appears to be driving the behavior from beneath the surface.

The next step in this journey requires us to explore that hidden space. It requires us to look beneath the addiction, beneath the substitute, and beneath the search itself.

It requires us to examine the vacancy beneath the behavior.
