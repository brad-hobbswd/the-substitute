Chapter 3

What Are We Really Trying to Escape?

Many addictions appear to involve the pursuit of pleasure. A closer look often reveals something deeper. This chapter explores the possibility that addiction is frequently connected to a desire to escape pain, quiet emotional burdens, and find relief from experiences that feel too difficult to carry alone.

"What if addiction is not merely a search for escape, but a search for relief from burdens that have become too heavy to carry?"

Once we begin viewing addiction through the lens of substitutes, another question naturally rises to the surface. What exactly is the substitute helping a person escape? While every story is unique and every individual brings a different history into the struggle, certain themes appear with remarkable consistency. Again and again, addiction seems connected to a desire to move away from something painful while moving toward something that feels better, safer, or easier to carry.

Most people assume addiction begins with the pursuit of pleasure. While pleasure may certainly play a role, the deeper motivation is often much more complicated. Many individuals are not primarily seeking excitement. Many are seeking relief. They are searching for a way to quiet something within themselves that feels overwhelming, exhausting, or impossible to manage.

The challenge is that emotional pain does not always announce itself clearly. Some forms of suffering are easy to recognize. Grief following a significant loss is obvious. Trauma resulting from abuse, violence, or major life disruptions often leaves visible marks on a person's story. Other forms of pain are much quieter. Years of rejection, chronic anxiety, loneliness, shame, insecurity, disappointment, emotional neglect, or feelings of inadequacy can shape a person's inner world without drawing much attention from others.

Over time, these burdens can become so familiar that a person no longer recognizes how much energy is being spent carrying them. The pain becomes part of daily life. The anxiety becomes normal. The loneliness becomes expected. The self criticism becomes an internal voice that never seems to rest.

Then something happens.

A person discovers a substance, behavior, or experience that changes how they feel.

Perhaps the anxiety becomes quieter for the first time in years. Perhaps emotional pain loses some of its intensity. Perhaps the constant pressure inside finally eases. Whatever the experience may be, the individual encounters something that provides temporary freedom from a burden that has felt permanent.

The relief can be powerful enough to create an immediate attachment. What began as an experience gradually becomes a strategy. The person learns that whenever the pain becomes too difficult, the substitute is available. Whenever loneliness grows stronger, the substitute offers comfort. Whenever fear becomes overwhelming, the substitute provides temporary escape.

This is why addiction often involves much more than a substance. Addiction frequently becomes a coping mechanism. The substitute is not simply consumed. The substitute is relied upon. It becomes a method of managing emotions, avoiding discomfort, and creating distance from experiences that feel unbearable.

Many family members struggle to understand this reality because they are focused on the visible consequences. From their perspective, the addiction appears to be creating the suffering. In many ways, that observation is absolutely correct. Addiction often produces tremendous damage. Yet from the perspective of the addicted individual, the addiction may have initially appeared to solve a problem rather than create one.

This distinction helps explain why logical arguments alone rarely produce lasting change. Family members often point to consequences, broken relationships, financial difficulties, health concerns, and countless other reasons to stop. The addicted person usually understands those consequences far better than anyone realizes. Knowledge alone, however, cannot compete with a coping mechanism that has become emotionally connected to survival.

The substitute feels necessary because the pain feels unbearable.

Understanding this reality does not remove responsibility. Every individual remains accountable for choices and behaviors. At the same time, meaningful recovery often requires more than removing the substitute. Recovery frequently involves helping a person develop healthier ways of facing what they have been trying to escape.

That process can be difficult because many people have spent years avoiding emotions they do not know how to manage. Grief that was never processed may finally demand attention. Trauma that remained buried may need to be addressed. Anxiety that was temporarily numbed may need proper treatment. Shame that shaped an individual's identity may need to be challenged and replaced with healthier beliefs.

The work of recovery therefore involves more than saying no to a substance. Recovery often requires saying yes to honesty. It requires acknowledging pain that has been ignored, fears that have been avoided, and wounds that have remained hidden beneath the surface.

As I continued listening to stories from both addicted individuals and family members, I noticed another important pattern. While many people were clearly trying to escape pain, they were also searching for something positive. Relief was only part of the equation. Beneath the desire to escape existed another longing that seemed equally powerful.

People were searching for peace.

The more I reflected on this idea, the more I began to wonder whether many addictions were not only attempts to escape suffering but also attempts to experience a sense of peace that felt unavailable anywhere else.

That possibility opens the door to an even deeper question. What if addiction is not merely a search for escape? What if addiction is also a search for peace?
