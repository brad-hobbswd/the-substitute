Chapter 6

When the Wrong Medication Becomes the Right Solution

Many people struggling with addiction are not simply pursuing pleasure. Many are searching for relief from anxiety, trauma, emotional pain, or other burdens that feel impossible to manage. This chapter explores how unhealthy solutions often begin as attempts to address very real problems.

"What if the addiction began because the wrong medication temporarily solved a very real problem?"

One of the most difficult realities surrounding addiction is that many people spend years attempting to solve legitimate problems with unhealthy solutions. The desire for relief is real. The suffering is real. The exhaustion is real. Yet the method chosen to address those struggles often creates consequences that eventually become as painful as the original problem itself.

As I listened to stories from individuals in recovery and family members affected by addiction, I began noticing something that rarely received enough attention. Many people were not simply chasing pleasure. Many were trying to feel normal.

That distinction matters.

The popular image of addiction often involves someone pursuing excitement, euphoria, or reckless enjoyment. While such situations certainly exist, countless people describe a very different experience. They were not looking to get high. They were looking for quiet. They were not chasing a thrill. They were searching for relief. They were not attempting to escape reality entirely. They were trying to make reality feel manageable.

Consider the individual living with severe anxiety that has never been properly addressed. Every day feels like a battle against racing thoughts, endless worry, and a nervous system that refuses to rest. When alcohol temporarily creates calm, the attraction is understandable. The alcohol may be destructive, yet the peace feels real.

Consider someone struggling with untreated ADHD who has spent years feeling overwhelmed, disorganized, impulsive, and frustrated. Life feels harder than it appears to be for everyone else. Criticism becomes familiar. Self confidence erodes. The individual discovers something that temporarily changes how they feel, and the relief creates an attachment that becomes increasingly difficult to break.

Consider a person carrying unresolved trauma who cannot understand why certain memories, emotions, or situations continue affecting daily life. Sleep becomes difficult. Emotional reactions seem unpredictable. Fear appears without warning. The person eventually discovers a substance or behavior that temporarily dulls those experiences. Once again, the attraction is not necessarily the substance itself. The attraction is relief from suffering.

These examples reveal an important truth. The wrong medication often begins as an attempt to solve a real problem.

The tragedy is that the solution creates new problems while leaving the original struggle largely unchanged. The anxiety remains. The trauma remains. The emotional wounds remain. The addiction gradually adds another layer of suffering on top of what already existed.

This is why proper understanding matters so much.

Imagine spending years treating a symptom while never identifying the condition causing it. Relief may come temporarily, yet lasting healing remains out of reach because the underlying issue continues operating beneath the surface. Many individuals find themselves trapped in exactly this situation. They know something feels wrong. They know life feels harder than it should. What they do not know is why.

For some people, a turning point occurs when an underlying struggle is finally recognized. A proper diagnosis may explain years of confusion. Counseling may reveal emotional wounds that have never healed. Trauma therapy may uncover connections between past experiences and present behaviors. Medical treatment may provide support that was missing for years. Recovery suddenly becomes more effective because the entire picture is finally being addressed.

This chapter is not intended to suggest that medication alone solves addiction. Recovery is far too complex for such a simple conclusion. Medication can be valuable when prescribed and monitored appropriately, yet medication cannot heal every wound, restore every relationship, or answer every emotional and spiritual question. Healing often requires a combination of support, treatment, honesty, accountability, community, and personal growth.

At the same time, ignoring legitimate mental health struggles can create unnecessary obstacles to recovery. A person battling addiction while simultaneously carrying untreated anxiety, ADHD, depression, trauma, or obsessive thinking may feel as though they are fighting multiple wars at once. Progress becomes significantly more difficult when major portions of the struggle remain unrecognized.

This realization helped me understand addiction in a new way. Rather than viewing every addictive behavior as evidence of weakness or poor choices alone, I began considering the possibility that some people were attempting to medicate pain they could not explain. The medication happened to be destructive, yet the need for relief was entirely real.

Such understanding does not remove accountability. Every person remains responsible for decisions and actions. What understanding does provide is compassion. It allows us to see the human being beneath the behavior. It reminds us that people often make unhealthy choices while trying to solve problems they do not know how to address.

The more I reflected on these realities, the more I realized that addiction never affects only the individual struggling with the substance. Every attempt to find relief creates consequences that ripple outward into the lives of parents, spouses, partners, children, siblings, and friends. While much attention is rightly given to the addicted person's recovery, another group of people often carries wounds that remain largely invisible.

Those individuals are the family members who love someone caught in addiction.

Their story deserves attention as well because addiction leaves marks on every life it touches.
